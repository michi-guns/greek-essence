# Greek Essence — Client Alignment Brief (M7)

Two deliverables: **what we need from the client** (and what we can fake ourselves, at a cost),
and **an executive brief** that explains the asks and the timeline to a non-technical person.

---

## Part 1 — The asks inventory

Every item follows the same shape, because the shape is the argument:

| Field | Why it exists |
|---|---|
| **What** | The thing itself, in her words |
| **Why** | What it is for, in one sentence, no jargon |
| **Blocks** | The specific work that cannot start without it |
| **Cost of delay** | In days. An ask without a number is a suggestion |
| **If she can't** | Our substitute, and honestly what it costs in quality |

That last column is the point of this exercise. Almost everything has a fallback — stock photos
instead of her photography, drafted copy instead of her voice, a template privacy policy instead
of a reviewed one. **Each fallback is worse in a way she should get to choose**, rather than one
we quietly pick for her. Listing them turns a demand into a decision.

Items to inventory (from the open questions in 00-DECISIONS Part D and the M6 content track):
domain and DNS · Google account for Forms and the Sheet · business inbox · what a package
actually is (the D-030 menu) · the launch destinations and packages · pricing stance ·
photography · copy approval · trust claims and evidence · privacy and terms · response-time
expectations.

---

## Part 2 — The executive brief

### What it has to do
Make a non-technical client feel that serious work is underway, understand exactly what only he
can provide, and accept a timeline without feeling it is an excuse.

### Five things worth stealing

**1. Stripe's annual letter — restraint.**
One number, not a dashboard. The premium feeling comes from what *isn't* on the page: no KPI
grid, no icon row, no stock photography. Confidence reads as leaving things out.

**2. Basecamp's *Shape Up*, specifically "Set Boundaries" — the appetite inversion.**
The single most useful idea for this document. Instead of *"how long will it take?"* it asks
*"how much time is this worth?"* — fixed time, variable scope. That reframe hands the client
control: the eight weeks stop being a cost imposed on him and become a budget he is spending.
It also makes every "can we add…" conversation trivial, because the answer is "yes, and here is
what comes out."

**3. Berkshire Hathaway's shareholder letters — authority through candour.**
Almost no design at all. Names what went wrong before what went right. This is the direct
antidote to AI slop, which hedges everything into mush. Say the awkward things plainly: *without
photographs by week 2, the launch moves.*

**4. Amazon's six-pager — the bullet ban.**
Narrative memos instead of slide bullets, because bullets let you skip the thinking. **A grid of
bulleted "benefits" is the number one tell of a generated document.** Full sentences force real
arguments and read as human.

**5. An architect's client brief — "you are paying for the drawings before the building."**
Exactly our M1 and M2 problem. Nobody questions why an architect spends weeks before a wall
goes up. The same framing makes the design system and the content platform legible as
foundations rather than delay.

### Anti-slop rules, concretely

| Never | Instead |
|---|---|
| A row of icon + heading + two lines | One idea per paragraph, in prose |
| "Leverage", "seamless", "robust", "empower" | Plain words a travel agent would use |
| A KPI grid of four vanity numbers | One number that carries the argument |
| Hedged phrasing that commits to nothing | A date, a name, a consequence |
| Generic stock imagery | Nothing, or the real thing |
| Emoji section headers | Typography doing the work |

### Unconventional components to earn the premium feel

- **A critical-path ribbon** — a horizontal band of the eight weeks with *his* dependencies
  marked in a colour that stands out. He sees instantly that the schedule waits on him twice.
- **"Decisions already taken"** — a count, then three examples with the reasoning. Around fifty
  decisions are recorded in `docs/v1/`. That is a true and unusual flex: most agencies show a
  client a mood board at this stage, not a decision register.
- **"What you will own"** — framed as assets in his name: his Sanity account, his Google account,
  his domain, a brand system, a site he can edit without calling anyone.
- **A two-column consequence table** — *if this arrives by Friday / if it arrives in three weeks*,
  with the launch date moving visibly in the second column.
- **"Already built"** — the repository exists, the foundation is done, checks pass. Concrete
  proof that the invoice precedes nothing.
- **A single closing ask** — one page, one list, his name against each item and a date.

### Format
A published web page rather than a PDF: it can be opened on a phone, shared with a partner, and
updated as items are ticked off. A PDF export follows if he asks for something to print.

### Tone
Written as though from a senior consultant who respects his time and is not trying to impress
him with vocabulary. Warm, direct, slightly understated. The confidence comes from specificity —
names, dates, numbers — never from adjectives.
